Module 1Lectures 01–02

Foundations of Reliability & Descriptive Statistics

Reliability and related terms

  • Reliability (IEC): the capability of a product, system or service to perform its expected job under specified conditions of use over an intended period of time.
  • Probabilistic definition (used to quantify it): the probability that a product performs its intended function without failure, under the stated conditions of use, over the stated period of time. In terms of the lifetime $T$: $R(t) = P(T \gt t)$.
  • Failure: an event or inoperable state in which an item does not perform as specified. A critical failure is one that can cause injury, major damage, or loss of the system's main function.
  • Three pillars: (1) the expected job, done within performance limits (this defines what counts as failure), (2) the conditions of use, (3) the mission time. Change any one of them and the reliability value changes.
  • Quality vs. reliability: quality is conformance to specification at time zero. Reliability is quality over time.
  • Failure mode is how an item fails (e.g. short circuit). Failure mechanism is the physical why (e.g. fatigue, corrosion).
  • Maintainability (also called serviceability): the time or resources needed to fix a failure. Quantitatively, it's the probability that a failed item is restored within a given time, summarised by MTTR.
  • Availability: the probability that the item is operational and ready to use when needed. As taught in the course: $A = \dfrac{\text{actual working time}}{\text{scheduled working time}} \times 100\%$. The equivalent steady-state formula is $A = \dfrac{MTTF}{MTTF + MTTR}$ (MTBF is used for repairable items).
  • Dependability is the umbrella term covering reliability, availability, maintainability and safety.

Descriptive statistics

  • Population (all items, described by parameters such as $\mu, \sigma$) vs. sample (the items observed, described by statistics such as $\bar{x}, s$).
  • Descriptive statistics summarise data. Inferential statistics draw conclusions about the population from a sample.
  • Variable types: qualitative (nominal, ordinal) and quantitative (discrete, continuous). The levels of measurement are nominal → ordinal → interval → ratio.
  • Central tendency: the mean uses every value but is pulled by outliers. The median is robust to outliers. The mode is the most frequent value and is the only one that works for nominal data.
  • Dispersion: range, variance $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2$, standard deviation, IQR $= Q_3 - Q_1$, and coefficient of variation $CV = s/\bar{x}$ (unit-free, so you can compare spreads across scales).
  • Shape: in right (positive) skew, mean > median > mode. In left skew the order reverses.
  • Boxplot: built from the five-number summary. In the lecture the whiskers simply run to the minimum and maximum. The standard (Tukey) convention, used by most software, also flags outliers beyond $Q_1 - 1.5\,IQR$ or $Q_3 + 1.5\,IQR$.
⚠️ Traps
  • Reliability is a probability tied to a time period. "Reliable" with no time and no conditions attached is meaningless.
  • Failures right after delivery are early-life (DFR) failures, not "random" ones, even if they look random.
  • With outliers or skewed lifetime data, report the median and IQR, not the mean and SD.
  • The mean time to failure is not the "typical" life of a unit. Under the exponential model only about 37% of units survive past the MTTF.

Probability Theory & System Reliability

Sets and sample spaces

  • Random experiment: an experiment whose outcome is uncertain but whose possible outcomes are known in advance. The sample space $S$ is the set of all outcomes. An event is a subset of $S$.
  • Mutually exclusive events: $A \cap B = \emptyset$. Exhaustive events: their union is $S$. A partition is both.
  • De Morgan's laws: $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$ and $(A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c$.
  • Approaches to probability: classical (equally likely outcomes), relative frequency (long-run proportion), subjective, and axiomatic (Kolmogorov).

Counting rules

  • Multiplication principle: if one step can be done in $n_1$ ways and the next in $n_2$ ways, both together can be done in $n_1 \times n_2$ ways.
  • Permutations (order matters): ${}^nP_r = \dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}$. Arrangements of $n$ objects with $n_1, \dots, n_k$ alike: $\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_k!}$.
  • Combinations (order doesn't matter): ${}^nC_r = \binom{n}{r} = \dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$.
  • Classical probability = favourable outcomes / total outcomes, both counted with these rules.

Axioms and rules

Kolmogorov's axioms
(1) $P(A) \ge 0$   (2) $P(S) = 1$   (3) for mutually exclusive $A_1, A_2, \dots$: $P(\bigcup A_i) = \sum P(A_i)$.
Consequences: $P(\emptyset) = 0$, $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$, $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow P(A) \le P(B)$, and $P(A) \le 1$.
  • Addition rule: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
  • Conditional probability: $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$ with $P(B) \gt 0$. It shrinks the sample space to $B$.
  • Multiplication rule: $P(A \cap B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$.
  • Independence: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$, which is equivalent to $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. If $A, B$ are independent, so are $A^c$ and $B$, $A$ and $B^c$, and $A^c$ and $B^c$.
  • Mutual vs. pairwise independence: for 3+ events, mutual independence needs every sub-collection to factor. Pairwise independence alone is not enough.

Total probability and Bayes

Law of total probability & Bayes' theorem
If $B_1, \dots, B_k$ partition $S$: $\;P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$.
$P(B_j \mid A) = \dfrac{P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}{\sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}$, so posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.
  • Prior $P(B_j)$ is your belief before the evidence. Likelihood $P(A \mid B_j)$ is how probable the evidence is under each cause. Posterior $P(B_j \mid A)$ is the updated belief. Bayes reasons from effect back to cause, which is why the course calls it reverse probability.

System reliability (reliability block diagrams)

  • Standard assumption: components fail independently. Every product formula below depends on it.
  • Series: the system works only if all $n$ components work. $R_s = \prod R_i$, so $R_s \le \min_i R_i$. Adding components can only lower it.
  • Parallel (active redundancy): the system works if at least one component works. $R_p = 1 - \prod (1 - R_i)$, so $R_p \ge \max_i R_i$. Each extra unit adds less than the one before (diminishing returns).
  • k-out-of-n (identical components): $R = \sum_{i=k}^{n} \binom{n}{i} R^i (1-R)^{n-i}$. Series is $n$-out-of-$n$ and parallel is 1-out-of-$n$.
  • Series-parallel: reduce the diagram block by block, replacing each pure series or parallel block with its equivalent reliability.
Must-know distinction
Mutually exclusive means the events cannot happen together ($P(A \cap B) = 0$). Independent means one happening tells you nothing about the other ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$). If $P(A), P(B) \gt 0$, mutually exclusive events are always dependent.
⚠️ Traps
  • $P(A \mid B) \ne P(B \mid A)$. Swapping them is the classic Bayes mistake.
  • Redundancy only helps if failures are independent. A common-cause failure (e.g. one shared power supply) breaks the parallel formula.
  • A series system is no more reliable than its least reliable component, and strictly less whenever any other component has $R \lt 1$.
Module 3Lectures 07–11

Discrete Random Variables & Distributions

Random variables

  • Random variable: a function that assigns a real number to each outcome in $S$. It's discrete if it takes countably many values.
  • PMF: $p(x) = P(X = x)$, with $p(x) \ge 0$ and $\sum p(x) = 1$.
  • CDF: $F(x) = P(X \le x)$. It's non-decreasing and right-continuous, with $F(-\infty) = 0$ and $F(\infty) = 1$. For a discrete variable it's a step function.
  • Expectation: $E[X] = \sum x\,p(x)$, the long-run average. It's linear: $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$, and $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$ always holds.
  • Chebyshev's theorem: for any distribution and any $k \gt 1$, $P(\mu - k\sigma \lt X \lt \mu + k\sigma) \ge 1 - \dfrac{1}{k^2}$. It's a conservative lower bound (e.g. at least 75% of values lie within $2\sigma$).
  • Variance: $Var(X) = E[(X-\mu)^2] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$, with $Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var(X)$. Adding a constant doesn't change spread. For independent $X, Y$: $Var(X \pm Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)$.

Key results
  • Poisson approximation to the binomial: as $n \to \infty$ and $p \to 0$ with $np = \lambda$ fixed, $B(n,p) \to \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$. Course rule of thumb: use it when $n \gt 10$ and $p \lt 0.1$ (both must hold).
  • Binomial approximation to the hypergeometric: works when $N$ is very large compared with $n$ (a common rule of thumb is $n/N \le 0.05$), because removing items then barely changes $p$.
  • Memoryless property: $P(X \gt m + n \mid X \gt m) = P(X \gt n)$. The geometric is the only discrete distribution with this property.
  • The factor $\frac{N-n}{N-1}$ is the finite-population correction. It makes the hypergeometric variance smaller than the binomial one.
⚠️ Traps
  • Poisson is the one whose mean equals its variance. If the data's variance is much larger than its mean (overdispersion), Poisson is a poor fit.
  • Check whether the geometric is defined as "trials until first success" (mean $1/p$) or "failures before first success" (mean $(1-p)/p$).
  • Sampling without replacement from a small lot means hypergeometric, not binomial.
Module 4Lectures 12–15

Continuous Distributions & Hazard Modelling

Continuous random variables

  • PDF $f(x)$: $f(x) \ge 0$, $\int f = 1$, and $P(a \lt X \lt b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. $f(x)$ itself is a density, not a probability, and can exceed 1.
  • For a continuous variable, $P(X = x) = 0$, so $\lt$ and $\le$ give the same probability.
  • $F'(x) = f(x)$.

The reliability functions and how they connect

Core relationships (lifetime $T \ge 0$)
$R(t) = 1 - F(t) = P(T \gt t)$,   $f(t) = -R'(t)$,   $h(t) = \dfrac{f(t)}{R(t)} = -\dfrac{d}{dt}\ln R(t)$
$R(t) = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t h(u)\,du\right) = e^{-H(t)}$,   $MTTF = E[T] = \int_0^\infty R(t)\,dt$
  • $R(t)$ starts at $R(0) = 1$, never increases, and goes to 0 as $t \to \infty$.
  • Hazard $h(t)$: the instantaneous failure rate given survival to t. $h(t)\,\Delta t \approx P(t \lt T \le t + \Delta t \mid T \gt t)$. Its shape (DFR / CFR / IFR) is what the bathtub curve plots.
  • Knowing any one of $f, F, R, h$ determines all the others.
  • Median life $t_{0.5}$ solves $R(t) = 0.5$. For right-skewed lifetimes it differs from the MTTF.

  • Exponential memoryless property: $P(T \gt s + t \mid T \gt s) = P(T \gt t)$. A used unit is "as good as new". It's the only continuous memoryless distribution, and constant hazard is the same statement.
  • Exponential: β or λ? If the question gives a mean life / MTTF, that's $\beta$. If it gives a failure rate (e.g. 0.0025 per hour), that's $\lambda = 1/\beta$.
  • Poisson ↔ exponential ↔ gamma: for events occurring at rate $\lambda$, the number of events in time $t$ is Poisson, the time to the first event (or between events) is exponential, and the time to the $k$-th event is gamma.
  • Normal approximation to the binomial: when the binomial is roughly bell-shaped, use $\mu = np$, $\sigma^2 = npq$ with a continuity correction of ±0.5. For example, $P(X \le 30)$ becomes $P(Y \le 30.5)$ and $P(X \lt 30)$ becomes $P(Y \le 29.5)$.
  • Weibull parameters not examined: Lec 13 and 15 say no exam questions come from Weibull: $\beta$ is the shape ($\lt 1$ DFR, $= 1$ exponential, $\gt 1$ IFR, $= 2$ Rayleigh, $\approx 3.5$ near-normal). $\theta$ is the scale or characteristic life: $R(\theta) = e^{-1}$, so 63.2% have failed by $\theta$ (with $\gamma = 0$). $\gamma$ is the location, i.e. a failure-free period.
  • Linear combinations of independent normals are normal: for $C = X_2 - X_1$, $\mu_C = \mu_2 - \mu_1$ and $\sigma_C^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2$. This is the basis of the clearance problem: a ball in a tube (bearing), with $C$ = tube inner diameter − ball diameter, and interference when $C \lt 0$.
⚠️ Traps
  • $h(t)$ is a rate, not a probability, so it can be larger than 1.
  • For a difference of independent variables the variances still add. Never subtract them.
  • Under the exponential, $R(MTTF) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368$. Only about 37% of units survive past the mean life.
  • Exponential means a constant hazard rate, not a constant number of failures per period. The number still failing falls as fewer units are left alive.

Sampling Distributions & the Central Limit Theorem

Sampling concepts

  • Random sample: $X_1, \dots, X_n$ independent and identically distributed (iid).
  • Sampling distribution: the probability distribution of a statistic (e.g. $\bar{X}$) over all possible samples of size $n$. Its standard deviation is the standard error.
  • $E[\bar{X}] = \mu$ and $Var(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2 / n$, so $SE(\bar{X}) = \sigma / \sqrt{n}$. Precision improves as $\sqrt{n}$: to halve the SE, you need 4× the sample.
  • Finite population correction beyond lectures: when sampling without replacement and $n/N \gt 0.05$, multiply the SE by $\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}$. The lectures assume an infinite population, or sampling with replacement.
  • If the population is normal, $\bar{X}$ is exactly normal for any $n$.
Central Limit Theorem
If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are iid with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, then as $n \to \infty$ $$\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1),$$ whatever the shape of the population. The usual rule of thumb is $n \ge 30$ (more for very skewed populations). It also applies to sums: $\sum X_i \approx N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$.

  • All three exact results assume a normal population. For normal samples, $\bar{X}$ and $S^2$ are independent.
  • F reciprocal property: $F_{1-\alpha}(\nu_1, \nu_2) = 1 / F_\alpha(\nu_2, \nu_1)$. Also $t_\nu^2 \sim F_{1,\nu}$.
  • Degrees of freedom: the number of independent pieces of information. $S^2$ has $n - 1$ because the deviations $x_i - \bar{x}$ must sum to zero, which costs one.
  • Two samples: $\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2$ has mean $\mu_1 - \mu_2$ and variance $\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}$. A sample proportion $\hat{p}$ has mean $p$ and variance $\frac{p(1-p)}{n}$, and is roughly normal when $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$.
⚠️ Traps
  • The CLT is about the distribution of the sample mean. It does not make the raw data normal.
  • The CLT needs finite variance and independence. Large $n$ alone isn't enough.
  • Use $Z$ when $\sigma$ is known. Use $t$ when $\sigma$ is estimated by $s$ and the population is normal, especially for small $n$.
  • Unlike $Z$ and $t$, the $\chi^2$ and $F$ distributions are not symmetric, so you need two different table values for a two-sided interval.

Point Estimation & Confidence Intervals

Estimators

  • An estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is a rule, so it's a random variable. An estimate is the number it gives for one particular sample.
  • Point estimation gives a single value. Interval estimation gives a range together with a confidence level.

Properties of a good estimator beyond lectures

PropertyDefinitionExample / note
Unbiased$E[\hat\theta] = \theta$. Bias $= E[\hat\theta] - \theta$$\bar{X}$ for $\mu$, and $S^2$ (divisor $n-1$) for $\sigma^2$. But $S$ is biased for $\sigma$.

$MSE(\hat\theta) = Var(\hat\theta) + \text{Bias}^2$. A slightly biased estimator can have a lower MSE than an unbiased one.
Cramér–Rao lower bound: any unbiased estimator has $Var(\hat\theta) \ge \dfrac{1}{n\,I(\theta)}$, where $I(\theta)$ is the Fisher information. An unbiased estimator that reaches the bound is efficient and is the MVUE.

Methods of estimation beyond lectures

  • Method of moments: set the sample moments equal to the population moments and solve. Simple, but not always efficient.
  • Maximum likelihood (MLE): $L(\theta) = \prod f(x_i; \theta)$. Choose the $\theta$ that maximises $\ln L$, usually by solving $\frac{d \ln L}{d\theta} = 0$. For the exponential: $\hat\lambda = n / \sum t_i = 1/\bar{t}$.
  • Properties of MLEs: consistent, asymptotically normal, asymptotically efficient (they reach the CRLB as $n \to \infty$), and invariant: the MLE of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$. They can be biased in small samples, e.g. the MLE of $\sigma^2$ divides by $n$.

What "95% confidence" means
If you repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true $\mu$. The 95% describes the method. Once an interval is computed, $\mu$ is either in it or not. $\mu$ is a fixed number, not a random one.
⚠️ Traps
  • "There is a 95% probability that μ lies in (40, 44)" is the wrong interpretation in frequentist statistics.
  • Unbiased doesn't mean consistent, and consistent doesn't mean unbiased. They are separate properties.
  • The variance CI relies on normality and is sensitive when that assumption fails.
  • Pairing on the same units removes unit-to-unit variation. Analysing paired data as two independent samples throws that advantage away.

Hypothesis Testing & Goodness-of-Fit

Framework

  • $H_0$ (null) is the status-quo claim and always contains the equality. $H_1$ (alternative) is the claim you're looking for evidence of. It can be one-tailed ($\lt$ or $\gt$) or two-tailed ($\ne$).
  • Test statistic: measures how far the data are from $H_0$, in standard-error units.
  • Significance level α: the false-rejection rate you'll tolerate. It sets the critical (rejection) region.
  • p-value: the probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. Reject $H_0$ if $p \le \alpha$.
$H_0$ true$H_0$ false
Reject $H_0$Type I error (probability α)Correct (power $= 1-\beta$)
Fail to rejectCorrect ($1-\alpha$)Type II error (probability β)
What drives power
Power goes up with a larger sample $n$, a larger true effect, a larger α, and a smaller $\sigma$. With $n$ fixed, lowering α raises β. With α fixed, increasing $n$ is the main practical way to lower β.
  • Why Type I error is controlled first: α is set directly by the tester. β depends on the unknown true value of the parameter, so it can only be computed for an assumed alternative.
  • OC curve and power curve (Lec 24): the OC curve plots β against the true μ. β is largest when the true μ is close to $\mu_0$ and falls as the difference grows. The power curve ($1-\beta$) approaches α as the true μ approaches $\mu_0$.
  • Tests on variance are usually one-tailed ($H_1: \sigma^2 \gt \sigma_0^2$), because variability measures consistency and only too much of it is a problem.
  • Non-parametric (distribution-free) tests are the fallback when the normality assumption doesn't hold (introduced in Lec 31).
  • Duality with CIs: a two-sided test at level α rejects $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ exactly when $\mu_0$ lies outside the $(1-\alpha)$ CI.

Chi-square tests for counts beyond lectures

  • Goodness-of-fit: asks whether the data follow a stated distribution. $\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O-E)^2}{E}$ with df $= k - 1 - m$ ($k$ categories, $m$ parameters estimated from the data).
  • Independence (r × c table): asks whether two categorical variables are related. $E_{ij} = \frac{\text{row}_i \times \text{col}_j}{\text{grand total}}$, df $= (r-1)(c-1)$. The course teaches this one in Lec 39 as the chi-square test of association (see Module 9).
  • Both tests are right-tailed, since large values mean a poor fit. Each expected count should be at least 5; merge categories if not.
⚠️ Traps
  • The p-value is not $P(H_0 \text{ is true})$, and it is not the probability the result happened "by chance".
  • "Fail to reject $H_0$" ≠ "accept $H_0$". Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.
  • Statistically significant ≠ practically important. With a huge $n$, tiny effects become significant.
  • Subtract 1 df for each parameter estimated in a goodness-of-fit test. beyond lectures
  • A Type I error happens when you reject. A Type II error happens when you fail to reject. Each one can only occur after its own decision.

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

Why ANOVA

  • Comparing $k$ means with pairwise $t$-tests needs $m = \binom{k}{2}$ tests. The family-wise error rate $\alpha_{FW} = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m$ grows quickly. ANOVA tests every mean together at a single α.
  • Despite its name, ANOVA tests means, by comparing two estimates of the variance.

Assumptions

  • (1) Independent observations, (2) normal errors within each group, (3) equal variances across groups (homoscedasticity). With equal group sizes (balanced design), the $F$-test is relatively insensitive to small departures from equal variances. With unbalanced data, the Type I error rate gets inflated and power drops.

One-way model

  • $y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \varepsilon_{ij}$, with $\sum \tau_i = 0$ and $\varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$. Course notation: $a$ treatments, $n$ replicates each, $N = an$ (some books write $k$ for $a$).
  • $H_0: \mu_1 = \cdots = \mu_a$. $H_1$: at least one mean differs.
Partitioning variation
$SS_T = SS_{Treatments} + SS_E$ (other books: $SST = SSB + SSW$), with df: $\;N - 1 = (a-1) + (N-a)$
$MS_{Treatments} = SS_{Treatments}/(a-1)$, $MS_E = SS_E/(N-a)$, $\;F_0 = MS_{Treatments}/MS_E \sim F_{a-1,\,N-a}$ under $H_0$ (right-tailed).
$MS_E$ is always an unbiased estimate of $\sigma^2$. $E(MS_{Treatments}) = \sigma^2$ only when $H_0$ is true; otherwise it's larger. That's why a large $F_0$ is evidence against $H_0$.
Computational formulas (they work for unbalanced data too), with $T_i$ = total of treatment $i$ and $T$ = grand total: $SS_T = \sum\sum y_{ij}^2 - \frac{T^2}{N}$,   $SS_{Treatments} = \sum \frac{T_i^2}{n_i} - \frac{T^2}{N}$.

Post-hoc comparisons named only, not taught

  • A significant $F$ only says that some difference exists. Post-hoc tests find which pairs differ.
  • The lecturer names three approaches: Bonferroni, Fisher's LSD and Tukey's HSD. She says finding which mean differs is beyond the scope of the lectures.
  • Bonferroni: run each of the $m$ pairwise tests at level $\alpha/m$.
  • Tukey's HSD: uses the studentised range $q$ and controls the family-wise error rate over all pairs. With equal $n$: $HSD = q_{\alpha,a,N-a}\sqrt{MS_E/n}$.
  • Fisher's LSD: pairwise $t$-tests using $MS_E$. Only valid after a significant $F$. Less conservative, so it has a higher family-wise error rate.

Two-way ANOVA named only, not taught

  • Two factors $A$ and $B$, giving main effects plus the interaction $AB$. An interaction means the effect of $A$ depends on the level of $B$ (on a plot, the lines are not parallel).
  • You need replication ($n \gt 1$ per cell) to estimate the interaction. The df are $a-1$, $b-1$, $(a-1)(b-1)$, and $ab(n-1)$ for error.
  • If the interaction is significant, interpret the main effects with care.
⚠️ Traps
  • $H_1$ is "at least one mean differs", not "all means differ".
  • ANOVA's $F$-test is always right-tailed.
  • With exactly 2 groups, one-way ANOVA is the same as the pooled $t$-test ($F = t^2$).

Correlation & Linear Regression

Correlation

  • Pearson's r $= \dfrac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}$ measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship.
  • Properties: $-1 \le r \le 1$. It has no units and is symmetric in $x$ and $y$. It's unchanged by shifting or positively rescaling either variable (a negative scale flips the sign). It's sensitive to outliers.
  • Testing $H_0: \rho = 0$: $t = \dfrac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}$ with $n-2$ df.
  • Which method for which data (Lec 38): Pearson for numeric (interval/ratio) data. It's parametric and assumes normal populations. Spearman for ordinal (ranked) data. It's non-parametric. Chi-square for nominal (categorical) data.
  • Spearman's $\rho_s$: Pearson's correlation applied to ranks (rank 1 = highest value). It measures monotonic association and resists outliers. Without ties, $\rho_s = 1 - \dfrac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}$.

Chi-square test of association (nominal data, Lec 39)

  • $H_0$: the two categorical variables are independent (not associated).
  • Expected count $E_{ij} = \dfrac{\text{row}_i\ \text{total} \times \text{column}_j\ \text{total}}{N}$, and $\chi^2 = \sum \dfrac{(O - E)^2}{E}$ with df $= (m-1)(n-1)$ for an $m \times n$ table.
  • The test is right-tailed: a large $\chi^2$ means association. Cells with large $(O-E)^2/E$ show which categories drive it. Expected counts should be at least about 5.

Simple linear regression

  • Model: $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon$. The lecture writes $y = \alpha + \beta x + \varepsilon$ with estimates $a, b$. Errors: $E(\varepsilon) = 0$, constant $\sigma^2$. Assumptions (LINE): Linearity, Independent errors, Normal errors, Equal error variance. $x$ is treated as fixed.
  • Least squares (OLS) minimises $\sum e_i^2$ by setting the partial derivatives to zero, giving slope $b = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ and intercept $a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}$.
  • Properties of the fitted line: it passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, the residuals sum to 0, and the residuals are uncorrelated with $x$.
Gauss–Markov theorem beyond lectures
If the errors have mean zero, constant variance and are uncorrelated, OLS is BLUE: the Best (minimum-variance) Linear Unbiased Estimator. Normality is not required for this. It's needed only for the $t$ and $F$ inference.
  • Variation: $SST = SSR + SSE$. The lecture defines $R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{SSE}{SST}$, which equals $SSR/SST$ for least squares with an intercept. It's the fraction of variation in $y$ that the model explains. In simple regression, $R^2 = r^2$. ($R^2 \lt 0$ is only possible for a fit that isn't least squares with an intercept, or when it's evaluated on new data.)

Inference for regression beyond lectures

  • Standard error of estimate: $s_e = \sqrt{SSE/(n-2)}$. The $n-2$ is because 2 parameters were estimated.
  • Significance of the slope: $t = b_1/SE(b_1)$ with $n-2$ df, or the regression ANOVA $F = MSR/MSE$. In simple regression $F = t^2$.
  • CI for the mean response vs. PI for a new observation: the PI adds the error variance of a single unit (the extra "1 +" under the square root), so it's always wider. Both are narrowest at $x = \bar{x}$ and widen as you move away.

Multiple regression

  • $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + \varepsilon$. Least squares gives $k+1$ normal equations, solved as a linear system. (In matrix form, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{y}$.)
  • Polynomial (non-linear) regression (Lec 42): $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \cdots + \beta_r x^r$. Substitute $x_1 = x, x_2 = x^2, \dots$ and solve it as a multiple linear regression. It needs $n \ge r + 1$ data points.
  • $R^2$ never decreases when you add predictors or polynomial terms, even useless ones. The lecture chooses the model with the higher $R^2$, which always favours the bigger model. Adjusted $R^2$ $= 1 - (1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}$ penalises extra terms beyond lectures.
  • Multicollinearity beyond lectures: highly correlated predictors make $X^TX$ nearly singular. That inflates the standard errors and makes coefficients unstable. It's measured with VIF.
⚠️ Traps
  • $r = 0$ means no linear relationship. A strong curved relationship can still have $r \approx 0$.
  • Correlation does not imply causation. A lurking variable may drive both.
  • A high $R^2$ doesn't prove the model is right. Check the residual plots.
  • Extrapolating beyond the range of $x$ in the data is unreliable.
  • Regressing $y$ on $x$ gives a different line from regressing $x$ on $y$, even though $r$ is the same.
Module 10Lecture 44

Auto-Regression & Time Series for Reliability

Time-series basics

  • A time series is a set of observations ordered in time. Neighbouring values are usually dependent, which breaks the iid assumption behind ordinary regression.
  • Reliability use in the lecture: forecasting quantities such as daily system unavailability or failure counts from their own past values.
  • Two required properties (Lec 44, 48): the data must be stationary (the probability distribution doesn't change over time) and uniform (consecutive, evenly spaced observations with no missing periods).
  • Weak (covariance) stationarity, the usual working version: (1) constant mean, (2) constant variance, (3) the autocovariance depends only on the lag $k$, not on time $t$.
  • Transformations: lag $Y_{t-1}$, first difference $\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$, log difference $\ln Y_t - \ln Y_{t-1}$ (≈ proportional change), and percentage change. Differencing is how you remove a trend.
  • Autocovariance: $\gamma_k = Cov(X_t, X_{t+k})$. Autocorrelation (ACF), also called serial correlation: $\rho_k = \gamma_k/\gamma_0$, with $\rho_0 = 1$.
  • White noise: uncorrelated errors with zero mean and constant variance. It's the "random kick" $\varepsilon_t$.

Autoregressive models

  • AR(p): $X_t = c + \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p X_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$. Today depends linearly on the last $p$ values. The lecture writes $Y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Y_{t-1} + \cdots + \beta_p Y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$, with $\varepsilon_t$ Gaussian white noise and $\beta_0$ often taken as 0. So $c \leftrightarrow \beta_0$ and $\phi_i \leftrightarrow \beta_i$.
  • Uses: forecasting, and estimating dynamic causal effects.
AR(1): $X_t = c + \phi X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ beyond lectures
Stationary if and only if $|\phi| \lt 1$. With $\phi = 1$ it's a random walk, which is non-stationary.
$\mu = \dfrac{c}{1-\phi}$,   $\gamma_0 = \dfrac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}$,   $\rho_k = \phi^k$ (decays exponentially, and alternates in sign if $\phi \lt 0$).
  • Yule–Walker equations (named in the lecture, details left for self-study) link the AR coefficients to the autocorrelations: $\rho_k = \sum_i \phi_i \rho_{k-i}$. For AR(1), $\hat\phi = r_1$, the lag-1 sample autocorrelation.
  • Forecasting: $\hat{X}_{t+h} = \mu + \phi^h (X_t - \mu)$. Forecasts drift back toward the mean as $h$ grows, and the forecast-error variance grows with $h$.
  • Remaining useful life (RUL) beyond lectures: the time until a forecast degradation path first crosses a failure threshold. That needs a trending (non-stationary) model, e.g. an AR model on differenced data or with drift. A stationary AR forecast reverts to its mean and can't predict a threshold crossing.
⚠️ Traps
  • Stationarity needs $|\phi| \lt 1$, not $\phi \lt 1$. A value like $\phi = -1.2$ is also explosive.
  • The mean of a stationary AR(1) is $c/(1-\phi)$, not $c$.
  • Long-horizon forecasts are less certain, not more.

Logistic Regression & Binary Classification

Why not linear regression for a 0/1 outcome?

  • Predictions can fall outside $[0, 1]$.
  • The errors can't be normal, since each takes only two values for a given $x$.
  • The variance $p(1-p)$ changes with $x$, so the constant-variance assumption is violated.

The logistic model

  • Odds $= \frac{p}{1-p} \in [0, \infty)$. Logit $= \ln\frac{p}{1-p} \in (-\infty, \infty)$, which can safely be modelled as a function of $x$. The lecture also writes $p = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-(x-\mu)/s}}$ (location $\mu$, scale $s$), so $\beta_0 = -\mu/s$, $\beta_1 = 1/s$ and $p(\mu) = 0.5$.
  • $\text{logit}(p) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k$, which gives $p = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta^T\mathbf{x})}}$ (the sigmoid).
  • Reading the coefficients: $\beta_j$ is the change in log-odds per unit increase in $x_j$, with the other predictors held fixed. $e^{\beta_j}$ is the odds ratio, the multiplier on the odds. If the logit uses log base $b$ (the lecture also uses base 10), the multiplier is $b^{\beta_j}$. If $\beta_j \gt 0$, $p$ rises with $x_j$.
  • The effect on $p$ itself is not constant. It's biggest near $p = 0.5$.
  • Decision boundary: with threshold 0.5, predict class 1 when $\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta^T\mathbf{x} \gt 0$. That boundary is linear only when the logit is linear in $\mathbf{x}$. The logit can include terms like $x^2$ (Lec 48: "the logit is always linear in x" is false).
  • Types: binary (one or many predictors), multinomial (pick a reference/pivot class 0 and fit $n-1$ logits $\ln\frac{p_j}{p_0} = \boldsymbol\beta_j^T\mathbf{x}$), and ordinal (ordered classes, separated by thresholds).
  • Grouped data: the empirical logit is $\ln\frac{\text{successes}}{\text{failures}}$ per group. If a count is 0, add 1 to both the numerator and the denominator.

Estimation and model assessment

  • MLE with a Bernoulli likelihood $\prod p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}$. There's no closed form, so it's solved iteratively (Newton–Raphson / IRLS). The log-likelihood is concave, so any optimum is global. It's unique when the predictors aren't collinear and the classes aren't perfectly separated; with perfect separation the MLE doesn't exist. OLS is not used.
  • Wald test (Lec 46): $z = \hat\beta_j / SE(\hat\beta_j)$ tests one coefficient and gives a p-value and a CI $\hat\beta_j \pm z_{\alpha/2}SE$.
  • Deviance beyond lectures $D = -2\ln L$, which plays the role of SSE.
  • Likelihood-ratio test beyond lectures: $G = D_{null} - D_{model} \sim \chi^2$ with df equal to the number of predictors. It tests the whole model.
  • Pseudo-$R^2$ beyond lectures (e.g. McFadden: $1 - \ln L_{model}/\ln L_{null}$) is not the same as OLS $R^2$ and is typically smaller.

  • ROC curve: TPR against FPR as the threshold sweeps from 1 to 0. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. 0.5 means random guessing and 1 means perfect separation.
  • Raising the threshold never increases recall and usually increases precision. Lowering it does the opposite.
⚠️ Traps
  • With imbalanced classes (e.g. 1% failures), a model that always predicts "no failure" is 99% accurate and useless. Use recall, precision, F1 or AUC instead.
  • $e^{\beta}$ multiplies the odds, not the probability.
  • Logistic regression is "strictly not a classifier" (Lec 45). It models the probability of a class, and becomes a classifier only once you pick a cut-off such as 0.5.
  • In safety-critical reliability work, missing a real failure (FN) usually costs more than a false alarm, so prioritise recall.

Classifiers: Naive Bayes, k-NN & Support Vector Machines

Supervised classification

  • Learn a mapping from features $\mathbf{x}$ to a class label using labelled training data, then judge it on unseen test data (generalisation).
  • Supervised vs unsupervised: supervised learning uses labelled data. Unsupervised learning (e.g. clustering) has no labels and is not covered. Classes must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive.
  • Course taxonomy (Lec 49): statistical (Bayes), distance-based (k-NN), decision tree (named only), and "error-based" (SVM).
  • Overfitting: the model fits noise in the training data and does poorly on new data. Underfitting: the model is too simple to capture the pattern. This is the bias–variance trade-off.

Bayes and Naive Bayes

  • MAP rule: $\hat{y} = \arg\max_c P(c)\,P(\mathbf{x} \mid c)$. The evidence $P(\mathbf{x})$ is the same for every class, so it can be dropped. The Bayes optimal classifier beyond lectures has the lowest possible error rate if the true probabilities are known.
  • Course terminology: the lecture calls $P(c)$ the prior and $P(x_j \mid c)$ the "posterior" probability. Most textbooks call $P(x_j \mid c)$ the likelihood. Expect the lecture's wording in MCQs.
  • Naive assumption: features are conditionally independent given the class, so $P(\mathbf{x} \mid c) = \prod_j P(x_j \mid c)$. This cuts the number of probabilities to estimate enormously. The second assumption is that all attributes contribute equally to the outcome. Training time is $O(n \times k)$ for $n$ records and $k$ attributes.
  • Zero-frequency problem: a single zero count wipes out the whole product. The course fixes it with the m-estimate: $P(x_j = v \mid c) = \dfrac{n_c + m\,p}{n + m}$, where $n$ = records in class $c$, $n_c$ = those with $x_j = v$, $m$ = "equivalent sample size", and $p$ = prior estimate (often $1/k$ for $k$ possible values). Laplace smoothing, $\frac{n_c + 1}{n + k}$, is the special case $m = k$, $p = 1/k$.
  • Continuous attributes: either discretise them into bins, or use a pdf such as $N(\mu_{jc}, \sigma^2_{jc})$ within each class (Gaussian NB).
  • Strengths: fast, works with little data and many features. Weakness: the independence assumption is usually false, so the probabilities are poorly calibrated, though the class rankings are often still good.

k-Nearest Neighbours

  • Instance-based ("lazy"), non-parametric: no training step. It stores all the data and classifies a new point by a majority vote of its $k$ nearest neighbours. This makes prediction slow.
  • Distances: Euclidean $\sqrt{\sum (x_i - z_i)^2}$ and Manhattan $\sum |x_i - z_i|$ (Minkowski generalises both). Feature scaling is essential.
  • Choosing k: small $k$ gives low bias, high variance and overfitting. Large $k$ gives high bias and over-smooths. Choose $k$ using held-out labelled data. Course heuristics: an odd $k$ for two classes, $k$ not a multiple of the number of classes, and $k \approx \sqrt{N}$.
  • Curse of dimensionality: with many features, distance computations get expensive and all points end up roughly equally far apart. The lecture's remedy is dimensionality reduction (PCA) or feature selection.

Hard-margin SVM (linearly separable data)

  • Hyperplane: $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b = 0$, where $\mathbf{w}$ is perpendicular to it. The distance from a point to it is $\frac{|\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b|}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$.
  • Canonical form: $y_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1$ for every $i$, which gives a margin of $\frac{2}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$.
  • Primal problem: minimise $\frac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ subject to those constraints. It's a convex quadratic program, so there's a unique global optimum and no local minima.
KKT conditions & the dual

The lecture writes the Lagrange multipliers as $\lambda_i$ (here $\alpha_i$) and the primal and dual Lagrangians as $L_P$ and $L_D$. $\mathbf{w} = \sum \lambda_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$ is called the representer theorem.

  • Stationarity: $\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$
  • Primal feasibility: $y_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1 \ge 0$. Dual feasibility: $\alpha_i \ge 0$
  • Complementary slackness: $\alpha_i\left[y_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\right] = 0$, so $\alpha_i \gt 0$ only for points on the margin. Those points are the support vectors.
  • Dual: maximise $\sum_i \alpha_i - \frac12\sum_i\sum_j \alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x}_j$. The data appear only through dot products, which is what makes kernels possible.
  • Recover $b = y_s - \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_s$ from each support vector and average the results (that's the lecture's method). Classify with $\text{sign}\left(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{x} + b\right)$.

Soft-margin SVM named only, not taught

  • Slack variables $\xi_i \ge 0$ allow violations: $y_i(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i$. The objective becomes minimise $\frac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum\xi_i$.
  • C is the penalty for violations. Large $C$ gives a narrow margin and few violations, so it risks overfitting. Small $C$ gives a wide margin and tolerates violations, so it risks underfitting. As $C \to \infty$ it becomes the hard margin.
  • Box constraint $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$:
    • $\alpha_i = 0$: correctly classified, beyond the margin (not a support vector).
    • $0 \lt \alpha_i \lt C$: exactly on the margin ($\xi_i = 0$).
    • $\alpha_i = C$: inside the margin ($0 \lt \xi_i \le 1$) or misclassified ($\xi_i \gt 1$).

Kernels and extensions

  • Explicit φ-mapping and its problems (Lec 57): e.g. quadratic monomials take 3-D data to 6-D. The difficulties are choosing the mapping, the computational cost, and the explosion in dimensionality. This motivates the dual form and the kernel trick.
  • Kernel trick: map $\mathbf{x} \to \phi(\mathbf{x})$ into a higher-dimensional space where the classes become linearly separable. Since the dual needs only dot products, you compute $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \phi(\mathbf{x})^T\phi(\mathbf{z})$ directly and never compute $\phi$.
  • Mercer's theorem: $K$ is a valid kernel if it's symmetric and every Gram matrix it produces is positive semi-definite. Kernels can be combined: $aK_1 + bK_2$ ($a, b \gt 0$) and $K_1 K_2$ are also kernels.
  • Common kernels: linear $\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{z}$; polynomial $(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{z} + c)^d$; sigmoid $\tanh(\kappa\,\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{z} + c)$; RBF $\exp(-\gamma\lVert\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\rVert^2)$, whose feature space is infinite-dimensional. A large $\gamma$ gives a very wiggly boundary and overfits.
  • Cost: the problem is convex (global optimum). Training costs about $O(n^3)$ in the number of samples, and prediction depends on the number of support vectors, not on the dimension.
  • Multi-class (Lec 56): OVA (one-vs-all) trains $k$ classifiers and picks the one with the largest $|\delta|$. OVO (one-vs-one) trains $\binom{k}{2}$ classifiers and assigns class $i$ only if every $\delta_{ij}$ agrees. A linear multi-class SVM fails if the classes aren't linearly separable.
  • SVR named only, not taught: fits an $\varepsilon$-insensitive tube. Errors smaller than $\varepsilon$ cost nothing, and the support vectors lie on or outside the tube.
