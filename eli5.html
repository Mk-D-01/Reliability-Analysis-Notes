Module 1Lectures 01–02

🧸 What is "reliability"?

Big idea: reliability means the toy keeps working for as long as it's supposed to, in the conditions it's supposed to work in.
1.1 Reliability engineering
  • Why failures matter: a broken toy makes a kid sad. A broken plane or bridge can kill people, so engineers have to care.
  • Three reasons to care: safety, money (repairs cost a lot), and keeping customers happy.
  • Definition: the chance the toy works (1) without breaking, (2) for a set amount of time, (3) under the conditions it was designed to be used in. Those are the three pillars. Reliability is a probability, so it's always between 0 and 1.
  • Related words:
    • MTTF (mean time to failure): how long a toy lasts, on average, before it breaks.
    • MTTR (mean time to repair): how long a fix takes, on average.
    • Availability $A = \frac{MTTF}{MTTF + MTTR}$: the share of time the toy is actually playable.
  • Bathtub curve: a toy's chance of breaking changes over its life, and the graph is shaped like a bathtub:
    • Baby phase (DFR, decreasing failure rate): badly made toys break early. The rate starts high and goes down.
    • Grown-up phase (CFR, constant failure rate): breaks happen only by random accident. The rate stays flat.
    • Old phase (IFR, increasing failure rate): the toy wears out. The rate goes up.
1.2 Statistics basics
  • Statistical thinking: lots of countries collected factory data. Japan's mid-20th-century success came from managers actually analysing that data with statistics, not just collecting it.
  • Population vs. sample: the population is every toy ever made. A sample is the 20 toys you actually check.
  • Types of variables: numbers you count (scratches), numbers you measure (weight), and categories (color).
  • Pictures of data: histograms (bar towers), stem-and-leaf plots, and boxplots.
  • Center: mean (the average), median (the middle toy when you line them up), mode (the most common value).
  • Spread: range, variance, and standard deviation tell you how scattered the toys are.
  • 5-number summary: min, Q1, median, Q3, max. These numbers draw a boxplot. In the lecture the whiskers just reach the smallest and biggest values. Many books also call a point beyond $Q_3 + 1.5\,IQR$ or below $Q_1 - 1.5\,IQR$ an outlier, a weird toy.
Module 2Lectures 03–06

🎲 Probability and how systems are connected

Big idea: probability measures how likely something is. Then we use it to find out whether a machine built from many parts will work.
2.1 Sets and sample spaces
  • Experiment: something with an uncertain result, like rolling a die.
  • Sample space: every possible result, {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
  • Event: a group of results you care about, like "an even number".
  • Set operations: union (A or B), intersection (A and B), complement (not A).
2.2 Probability rules (Kolmogorov's axioms)
  • A probability is never negative.
  • Something in the sample space has to happen, so P(everything) = 1.
  • If two events can't happen together, you add their probabilities.
  • Addition rule: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. You subtract the overlap so it isn't counted twice.
2.3 Conditional probability and independence
  • Conditional probability: "given that it's raining, how likely is a traffic jam?" Knowing one thing changes the odds of another.
  • Independent events: one doesn't affect the other at all, like two separate coin flips. Then $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$.
2.4 Total probability and Bayes' theorem
  • Total probability: toys come from 3 factories. To get the overall chance a toy is broken, add up each factory's share of the breakage.
  • Bayes' theorem works backwards: you found a broken toy, so which factory probably made it?
2.5 How the parts are connected
  • Series: a chain of holiday lights where one bad bulb makes everything go dark. $R = R_1 R_2 \cdots R_n$. The system is never stronger than its weakest part.
  • Parallel (active redundancy): you carry 2 flashlights, and you only lose light if both fail. $R = 1 - \prod (1 - R_i)$.
  • Series-parallel: a mix of the two. Simplify it one block at a time.
  • k-out-of-n: a plane with 3 engines that flies fine as long as any 2 work. You solve it with the binomial (Module 3).
Module 3Lectures 07–11

🔢 Counting-type random outcomes

Big idea: a random variable is a number that comes from chance, such as "how many broken toys are in this box".
3.1 Random variables
  • Discrete: you can count it (0, 1, 2, …).
  • PMF (probability mass function): the chance of each exact value.
  • CDF (cumulative distribution function): the chance of getting that value or less.
  • Expectation $E[X]$: the average you'd get if you repeated the experiment forever. Variance: how much results jump around that average.
3.2 Common distributions (pick the one that matches the story)

GeometricTries until the first successTest cycles until the first failure

  • Poisson approximation: when n is huge and p is tiny, the binomial looks like a Poisson with $\lambda = np$. The course's rule: use it when n > 10 and p < 0.1.
  • Memoryless (geometric): the toy doesn't "remember" how many tests it already survived. The chance it fails on the next test is the same as on the first.
Module 4Lectures 12–15

🌊 Smooth random outcomes and the failure rate

Big idea: some things, like time, are measured instead of counted, so you can get any value, such as 3.71 hours.
4.1 The four key reliability functions
  • PDF $f(t)$: how bunched up the failures are around time t.
  • CDF $F(t)$: the chance the toy has died by time t.
  • Reliability $R(t) = 1 - F(t)$: the chance the toy is still alive at time t.
  • Hazard rate $h(t) = f(t)/R(t)$: "this toy is still working right now, so how likely is it to break in the very next moment?" This is the line drawn on the bathtub curve.
  • MTTF $= \int_0^\infty R(t)\,dt$: average lifetime, which is the total area under the survival curve.
4.2 Distributions
  • Uniform: every value between a and b is equally likely, like a fair spinner.
  • Exponential: the failure rate λ never changes, so it describes the flat middle of the bathtub. $R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, MTTF $= \beta = 1/\lambda$. (If a question gives a mean life, that's β. If it gives a failure rate, that's λ.) It's memoryless: a used bulb is as good as a new one.
  • Normal: the bell curve. Most toys are close to average size and a few are far off.
    • Clearance problem: does the ball fit in the tube? Clearance = tube's inside width − ball's width. If that's below 0, the parts won't fit.
    • μ and σ from two percentiles: you know two "x% of toys are below this value" facts. Each one gives an equation, so you get two equations in two unknowns.
  • Gamma: how long until the k-th breakdown. The exponential is the special case "until the first breakdown".
  • Lognormal: the logarithm of the data is normal. In this course it's used for how long repairs take.
  • Weibull not in the exam: the shape-shifter. Its shape parameter β tells you where you are on the bathtub:
    • $\beta \lt 1$: early "baby" failures
    • $\beta = 1$: random failures (this is the exponential)
    • $\beta \gt 1$: wear-out
Module 5Lectures 16–21

🥄 Sampling and the Central Limit Theorem

Big idea: you can't test every toy, so you test a few. How much can those few tell you about all of them?
5.1 The sample mean and the CLT
  • Parameter vs. statistic: μ is the true average of all toys, and nobody knows it. $\bar{x}$ is the average of your sample, which you can calculate.
  • Distribution of $\bar{x}$: if you took many samples, their averages would cluster around μ, with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Bigger samples give tighter clusters.
  • Finite population correction (FPC) beyond lectures: a small fix when your sample is a big chunk of a small population.
  • Central Limit Theorem: take a big enough sample (roughly n ≥ 30) and the average behaves like a bell curve, even if the individual toys don't.
5.2 Chi-square (χ²) and the sample variance
  • χ² comes from adding up squared bell-curve values. You use it to ask how spread out the toys really are: $\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$.
5.3 The t-distribution and the F-distribution
  • t: like the bell curve but with fatter tails. Use it when the sample is small and you don't know σ. With more data it turns into the normal.
  • F: one variance divided by another. Use it to ask whether machine A is messier than machine B. Reciprocal property: $F_{1-\alpha}(a,b) = 1/F_{\alpha}(b,a)$.
5.4 Two samples
  • The distribution of the difference between two averages, or between two proportions. Use it to compare factory A with factory B.
Module 6Confidence intervals: Lectures 26–28

🎯 Making smart guesses (estimation)

Big idea: use your sample to guess the true number, then say how sure you are.
6.1 Point estimation (one best guess) beyond lectures
  • Estimator vs. estimate: an estimator is the recipe ("take the average"). An estimate is the number it gives you ("42").
  • What makes a good estimator:
    • Unbiased: on average it hits the true value. This is why the sample variance divides by n−1 instead of n.
    • Consistent: more data brings it closer to the truth.
    • Efficient (MVUE): among unbiased estimators, its guesses jump around the least. The CRLB is the smallest wobble any unbiased estimator can have.
    • Sufficient: it uses all the useful information in the data.
6.2 How to build an estimator beyond lectures
  • Method of moments: make the sample average equal the theoretical average, and solve for the unknown.
  • MLE (maximum likelihood): "which parameter value makes the data I actually saw the most likely?" For the exponential this gives $\hat{\lambda} = n / \sum t_i$.
6.3 Confidence intervals (a net, not a single spear)
  • What a CI is: "I'm 95% confident μ is between 40 and 44." If you repeated this recipe 100 times, about 95 of the nets would catch the true value.
  • CI for μ: use Z when σ is known or n is large, and t when σ is unknown and n is small.
  • Two means: pooled t (independent groups) or paired t (the same toys before and after).
  • Proportion p: $\hat{p} \pm Z\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$.
  • Variance σ²: uses χ², so the interval is lopsided, not symmetric around the estimate.
  • Sample size: $n = (Z\sigma/E)^2$ answers "how many toys do I need for my error to stay below E?"

⚖️ Hypothesis testing, or "prove it!"

Big idea: it works like a courtroom. You start by assuming nothing special is going on ($H_0$, "innocent") and only reject that if the evidence is very strong.
7.1 Basics
  • $H_0$ vs. $H_1$: $H_0$ says "the battery lasts 100 hours as claimed." $H_1$ says "no, it lasts less."
  • Test statistic: one number that measures how far the data are from what $H_0$ predicts.
  • Rejection region / p-value: if the result would be very unlikely when $H_0$ is true, you reject $H_0$.
  • The two kinds of mistake:
    • Type I (α): convicting an innocent person, i.e. saying there's an effect when there isn't.
    • Type II (β): letting a guilty person go, i.e. missing a real effect.
    • Power = 1 − β: how good the test is at catching a real effect.
    • Making α smaller makes β bigger unless you collect more data.
7.2 Which test do you use?
You want to test…Use
One mean, σ known or big nZ-test
One mean, small n, σ unknownt-test

Before and after on the same toysPaired t-test

7.3 Chi-square tests
  • Goodness-of-fit beyond lectures: "is this die fair?" Compare observed with expected counts: $\sum \frac{(O-E)^2}{E}$.
  • Independence (r×c table): "does the failure type depend on which shift made the toy?" Expected count = (row total × column total) / grand total. The course teaches this one in Lecture 39, alongside correlation.
Module 8Lectures 32–37

🍎🍊🍌 ANOVA, comparing many groups at once

Big idea: if you have 4 machines and want to know whether they make toys of the same average weight, you could run lots of t-tests, but that's a trap.
8.1 Why not run lots of t-tests?
  • Each test has a small chance of a false alarm, and those chances stack up: $\alpha_{FW} = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m$. With 6 tests at α = 0.05, the chance of at least one false alarm is about 26%.
  • ANOVA assumptions: each group is normal, the groups have equal variances, and observations are independent.
8.2 One-way ANOVA
  • Splitting the variation: $SST = SSB + SSW$. The lecture writes this as $SS_T = SS_{Treatments} + SS_E$.
    • SSB (between groups): how far apart the group averages are.
    • SSW (within groups): how much toys from the same machine wobble.
  • $F = MSB / MSW$. If groups differ much more than toys wobble inside a group, F is large, which is strong evidence that at least one machine is different. (The lecture writes F = MSTreatments / MSE.)
  • The ANOVA table columns are Source | SS | df | MS | F. You'll need to fill one in.
8.3 Post-hoc tests: which ones differ? named only
  • ANOVA only tells you that some group is different. Tukey's HSD and Fisher's LSD compare pairs to find out which groups differ.
8.4 Two-way ANOVA named only
  • Two factors, like machine and operator, plus an interaction: maybe operator Bob only struggles on machine 3.
Module 9Lectures 38–43

📈 Correlation and regression

Big idea: do two things move together, and can you draw a line that predicts one from the other?
9.1 Correlation
  • Pearson's r runs from −1 to +1:
    • +1: both go up together, in a perfect straight line
    • 0: no straight-line relationship
    • −1: one goes up while the other goes down
  • Testing r: checks whether the correlation is real or just luck in the sample.
  • Spearman's ρ: the same idea on ranks. Use it for ranked data like S/M/L sizes, or when the relationship is curved but always goes one direction.
  • Chi-square test: for plain categories (colour, shift, vaccinated or not). Compare the counts you got with the counts you'd expect if the two things had nothing to do with each other.
  • ⚠️ Correlation doesn't prove that one thing causes the other.
9.2 Simple linear regression
  • Model: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon$. For example, toy lifetime = start + slope × temperature.
  • OLS (ordinary least squares): choose the line that makes the sum of squared vertical misses as small as possible.
  • Gauss-Markov beyond lectures: under the standard assumptions, OLS is BLUE, the best linear unbiased estimator.
  • $R^2 = 1 - SSE/SST$: the share of the up-and-down variation in y that the line explains (0 to 1 for a least-squares line).
  • Standard error $s_e$ beyond lectures: the typical size of a miss.
  • Regression ANOVA and the slope test beyond lectures: checks whether the slope is really different from 0.
  • CI vs. PI beyond lectures:
    • A confidence interval is for the average toy at x, so it's narrower.
    • A prediction interval is for one new toy at x, so it's wider.
9.3 Multiple and polynomial regression
  • Several x's at once. Least squares gives one equation per coefficient (the "normal equations"), and you solve them together.
  • Polynomial regression: to fit a curve, use $x$, $x^2$, $x^3$, … as if they were separate x's, then solve it like multiple regression.
  • Bigger isn't always better: adding x's or powers never lowers $R^2$, so picking the model with the highest $R^2$ always favours the biggest one.
  • Adjusted $R^2$ beyond lectures penalizes you for piling on useless x's.
  • Multicollinearity beyond lectures: two x's that are near-twins confuse the model.
Module 10Lecture 44

⏳ Time series and the AR(1) model

Big idea: how much a machine is down this week tends to look like last week. Today's value depends on yesterday's. (The data have to be "steady" over time, called stationary, and evenly spaced with no gaps.)
10.1 Time-series basics
  • Examples: daily downtime of a system, number of failures per month, vibration readings.
  • Stationarity: the series looks "the same" statistically over time, with a steady mean and spread.
  • Autocovariance and ACF: how much today resembles yesterday, the day before that, and so on.
10.2 The AR(1) model
  • $X_t = c + \phi X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, meaning today = some of yesterday + a random kick.
  • It's stationary only when $|\phi| \lt 1$. Otherwise it either explodes ($|\phi| \gt 1$) or wanders off without settling ($|\phi| = 1$).
  • Mean $\mu = \frac{c}{1-\phi}$, variance $\gamma_0 = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi^2}$.
  • Autocorrelation at lag k: $\rho_k = \phi^k$, so the "memory" fades over time.
  • Yule-Walker: estimate φ from the data. For AR(1), $\hat\phi$ is just the lag-1 autocorrelation.
10.3 Forecasting and remaining useful life beyond lectures
  • Project the curve forward, find when it crosses the "broken" threshold, and that time is the remaining useful life (RUL).
Module 11Lectures 45–48

🚦 Logistic regression, predicting yes or no

Big idea: you want to predict whether the toy fails (1) or survives (0). A straight line can predict silly values like −0.3 or 1.4, so you need something else.
11.1–11.2 Odds, logit, and the sigmoid
  • Odds: $p/(1-p)$. "3 to 1 odds" means p = 0.75.
  • Logit: $\ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$. You fit a straight line on the log-odds scale.
  • Sigmoid: $p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1 x)}}$, an S-curve that always stays between 0 and 1.
  • Odds ratio $e^{\beta}$: each 1-unit increase in x multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta}$.
11.3–11.4 Fitting and checking the model
  • MLE: there's no closed-form answer, so the computer climbs toward the best β step by step.
  • Deviance and the likelihood-ratio test check the whole model. beyond lectures
  • The Wald test checks one predictor.
  • Pseudo-$R^2$ measures fit. beyond lectures
11.5 How good are the predictions? beyond lectures
  • Confusion matrix: a table of TP, FP, TN, FN.
    • Accuracy: how many predictions were right overall.
    • Precision: when it said "fail", how often was it right?
    • Recall (sensitivity): of all real failures, how many did it catch?
    • F₁: a balance of precision and recall.
  • ROC curve and AUC: test every possible cut-off and plot the results. AUC = 1 is perfect and 0.5 is a coin flip.
Module 12Lectures 49–60

🤖 Machine-learning classifiers

Big idea: teach the computer to sort toys into "good" and "faulty" by showing it labeled examples. That's supervised learning.
12.1 Naive Bayes
  • MAP rule: pick the class that's most likely given the clues.
  • The "naive" assumption: pretend every clue is independent of the others given the class. Usually false, but it still works surprisingly well.
  • m-estimate (the course's fix): blend each count with a small made-up "prior" count so a clue you've never seen doesn't zero out the whole product. Laplace smoothing (add 1 to every count) is the simplest version.
  • Gaussian NB: for measured features, model each one with a bell curve.
12.2 k-Nearest Neighbors
  • "Tell me who your neighbors are." To classify a new toy, find the k most similar toys and take a majority vote.
  • Distance: Euclidean (straight line), Manhattan (city blocks). Scale your features first so one big-number feature doesn't dominate.
  • Choosing k:
    • Small k is jumpy and overfits (high variance).
    • Large k blurs everything and underfits (high bias).
12.3 Hard-margin SVM
  • Draw a line between red toys and blue toys that leaves the widest possible empty street between them. The margin is $\frac{2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert}$.
  • Support vectors: the toys standing right on the edge of the street. They're the only ones that decide where the line goes.
  • Primal problem: minimize $\tfrac12\lVert \mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ while keeping every toy on the correct side.
  • Lagrange multipliers, KKT, and the dual: a math trick that rewrites the problem in terms of $\alpha_i$, one per toy. Only support vectors get $\alpha_i \gt 0$. You get b back from any support vector.
12.4 Soft-margin SVM named only
  • Real data is messy, so a few toys are allowed on the wrong side, with "slack" $\xi_i$.
  • C controls the trade-off:
    • Large C is strict and tolerates few mistakes.
    • Small C is relaxed and gives a wider street.
  • Box constraint: $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$. That gives three cases: off the margin, on the margin, or inside it / misclassified.
12.5 Kernels
  • When classes can't be split by a straight line, lift the toys into a higher dimension where a flat cut works. Picture flicking a tablecloth upward so red toys fly higher than blue ones.
  • Kernel trick: you never compute that lift. You only compute $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})$, which works like a similarity score.
  • Mercer's theorem: the condition a function must meet to be a valid kernel.
  • Common kernels: linear, polynomial, and RBF (Gaussian).
12.6 Beyond two classes
  • Multi-class: one-vs-all (OVA) or one-vs-one (OVO).
  • SVR named only: an SVM for predicting numbers. It fits a tube and ignores errors smaller than ε.

✅ Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open the answer.

  1. A system has 3 parts in series, each with R = 0.9. Is the system more or less reliable than 0.9, and why?
    Show answer

    Less: $0.9^3 = 0.729$. In series, every part has to work, so each extra part is one more way for the system to fail.

  2. A bulb has a constant failure rate and has already run for 1,000 hours. Is it more likely to fail in the next hour than a brand-new bulb?
    Show answer

    No. A constant failure rate means the exponential distribution, which is memoryless: $P(T \gt s+t \mid T \gt s) = P(T \gt t)$. The used bulb is "as good as new".

  3. You have 8 samples and σ is unknown. Z or t?
    Show answer

    t, with $n-1 = 7$ degrees of freedom. The sample is small and σ has to be estimated by s.

  4. Why can't you just run 6 t-tests instead of one ANOVA?
    Show answer

    The false-alarm chance stacks up: $1 - 0.95^6 \approx 0.26$. ANOVA tests all the means together at a single α.

  5. In an SVM, if you delete a toy that is not a support vector, does the line move?
    Show answer

    No. Only support vectors have a nonzero Lagrange multiplier ($\lambda_i \gt 0$ in the lecture's notation, $\alpha_i$ in many books), so they're the only points that set $\mathbf{w}$ and b.
